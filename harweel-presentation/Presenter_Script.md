### Slide 6 — Challenge 1 — the rock is tight
[≈ 35 sec]

**Visual / animation objective:**
Two 'microscope' lenses zoom in on the rock. Left: big grains with open channels — bright oil droplets stream continuously through them. Right: Harweel's tight rock — tiny grains packed closely; the oil only trembles in place. The contrast should be understood without words.

**Script:**
The first challenge is the rock itself. Imagine looking at it under a microscope. In a typical reservoir — on the left — the grains leave wide, connected spaces, and oil can flow through them toward a well. Harweel's rock, on the right, is very tight: the spaces between the grains are tiny and poorly connected, so the oil simply does not move through it easily.

**Accuracy notes:**
'The rocks are very tight and the oil does not move through them easily' is from the source. Grain and pore sizes are illustrative only.

### Slide 10 — The idea — put the gas back to work
[≈ 50 sec]

**Visual / animation objective:**
The hero slide — the whole process at once. Sequence: (1) the surface recycle pipe draws in and cyan gas particles flow along it, joined by Rabab gas; (2) gas streams down the injection well; (3) a cyan gas front slowly sweeps through the oil layer from right to left; (4) amber oil particles drift toward the production well and rise up it. A dashed 'lens' then marks the point we zoom into next.

**Script:**
Then came the idea. Instead of treating the sour gas as a problem, put it back to work. The gas that comes up with the oil is separated at the surface and injected back down into the reservoir — together with additional gas from the nearby Rabab field. Deep underground, that gas dissolves into the oil and helps it move toward the production wells, where it is brought back to the surface. So the challenge itself becomes part of the solution. Let's zoom in to see what 'dissolving' actually does.

**Accuracy notes:**
Re-injecting the sour gas plus gas from the Rabab field, the gas dissolving in the oil and helping it move to production wells — all from the source. The surface 'separation' wording is a simplification of how produced gas reaches the injection wells; well spacing and geometry are schematic.

### Slide 11 — Inside the rock: how gas frees the oil
[≈ 35 sec]

**Visual / animation objective:**
Morph zoom: the small dashed lens from the previous slide expands into a large magnified view of the reservoir rock. Cyan gas molecules drift in and fade as they dissolve; the oil-filled pores lighten (the oil becomes thinner); then oil droplets start moving through the rock toward the producer.

**Script:**
Here's what happens at the scale of the pores. The injected gas reaches the oil trapped in the tiny spaces between the grains. Instead of pushing past it, the gas dissolves into the oil. That makes the oil swell and become thinner and lighter — and a thinner fluid can squeeze through tight rock far more easily. That's how the gas helps the oil move toward the production wells.

**Accuracy notes:**
'The gas dissolves in the oil and helps it move to the production wells' is from the source. The explanation that dissolved gas swells the oil and reduces its viscosity is standard reservoir-engineering context added for clarity.

In 2014, full-scale injection began at Zalzala, and a year later it reached full capacity.
